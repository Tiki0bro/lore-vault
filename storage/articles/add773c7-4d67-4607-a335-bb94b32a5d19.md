The Lifesong 
To the Elves, magic is not a force to be commanded, but a melody to be joined . They call the Godpulse the 

Lifesong, the eternal harmony between life, death, and rebirth . For them, every living thing—trees, stars, rivers—holds a note in this grand chorus . Spellcraft is simply the act of learning to "hear" how the world sings and joining in its chorus .

Magical Practices
Elven magic is a learned art, much like music. An apprentice mage might start by learning to identify the most basic "notes" of the Lifesong—the simple hum of a rock or the sharp chord of a crackling fire . As they progress, they learn to read the "score" of the world, an intuitive understanding of how the Lifesong flows and changes .

Elven mages, often called "chanters," do not use incantations or gestures as other races might . Instead, they sing or hum complex melodies to manipulate the Lifesong's energy . This approach is unique because it's about synchronization rather than creation. A chanter would find a source of fire and sing a note that matches the flame's "song," amplifying it into a destructive chord. The result is a humming ball of fire that is released with a final, sharp note.

As a chanter's skill grows and their connection to the Lifesong deepens, they no longer need to rely on their vocal cords. They can "sing" in their minds and hearts, creating a purely internal melody that is even more focused and precise than a spoken one. This allows them to cast spells silently, which is an immense advantage in combat. They can cast spells without giving away their position or being vulnerable to a sonic attack.

Elven druids take this a step further, believing the forest itself sings . They learn to answer its call, using the energy around them to cast druidic magic .


Cultural Impact
The Lifesong shapes Elven society and its relationship with other races. The Elves value bloodline purity, which is reflected in their magic . This linguistic connection to magic makes Elven society deeply intertwined with the arts. The Elvish language itself is a form of musical notation, designed to mimic the harmonies and rhythms of the world's natural melodies . The word for "fire," for instance, would be a collection of syllables and tones that, when spoken, create a frequency that resonates with the natural song of fire, making it easier for a chanter to attune to it.


The Elves are wary of the Marike people, who also possess a nature-based magic but have a different understanding of it . Despite the cultural similarities, the Elves have attempted to make contact but have been largely rejected by the Marike . This cultural divide shows that even when two races share a similar magical philosophy, their interpretation can lead to significant differences.
